# S12 — Stability with and without a motor: narration script

Record one file per numbered cue, as `voice/NN.wav` (any sample rate, mono is fine).
Leave a beat of silence at the start and end of each. Re-run render.py and the slides
re-time themselves to your voice.

**01** `voice/01.wav`

Segment twelve. Stability with and without a motor. By the end of this you will be able to add a motor to the OpenRocket model, see what it does to CG and stability, run a simulation, and read apogee, rail-exit speed and the optimum delay.

**02** `voice/02.wav`

Open BR-1.ork with no motor configuration selected. Blue dot, CG. Red dot, CP. The same two dots from Segment ten. Empty, the CG sits twenty-four and three quarter inches, sixty-three centimeters from the nose tip, and the margin reads about two point nine calibers.

**03** `voice/03.wav`

Now add the motor most of you will fly. Motors and configuration, new configuration, G74W-6. The CG slides aft to twenty-six inches, sixty-six centimeters, as the string showed in Segment three. The CP stays put. The margin drops to two and a half calibers. On the pad, this is the least stable the rocket will ever be.

**04 — pause card, no narration.** The card reads: _On the pad with the motor loaded, is this rocket more stable or less stable than it will be at burnout? Why?_

**05** `voice/05.wav`

Less. Propellant is mass at the very back. As it burns off, the CG moves forward, away from the CP, and the margin grows through the burn. Put numbers on it. The G74 carries one point four ounces, thirty-nine grams of propellant, gone by burnout at one point two seconds. The CG moves forward to twenty-five and a quarter inches, sixty-four centimeters, and the margin climbs from two and a half calibers to two point seven.

**06** `voice/06.wav`

Here is OpenRocket's plot of the margin for the first two seconds. Two and a half on the pad, two point seven at burnout, then flat. After burnout the mass and the shape are fixed, so the margin holds until the parachute comes out.

**07** `voice/07.wav`

Now the motor some of you will certify on. Same clicks, H135W-8. It is more than twice the mass of the G, so the CG goes further aft, to twenty-seven and a half inches, seventy centimeters, and the margin drops to two, the answer from Segment five. That is why an L1 flight on the same airframe deserves a second look at the numbers, and sometimes a little nose weight.

**08** `voice/08.wav`

Run the G configuration and read four numbers. Apogee, about seven hundred feet, two hundred and ten meters. Top speed, about one hundred and fifty miles an hour, sixty-eight meters per second. Off the rail, fifty-four feet per second, sixteen and a half meters per second. Six point eight seconds to apogee. The six second delay fires at seven point two, and OpenRocket's best delay is five point seven, so six is right. Then the H. Twenty-one hundred and fifty feet, six hundred and fifty meters. Three hundred and forty miles an hour, one hundred and fifty meters per second. Sixty-eight feet per second, twenty and a half meters per second off the rail. Ten point four seconds up, best delay eight point six.

**09** `voice/09.wav`

Two more knobs, wind and rail angle. Here is the H, rail tilted three degrees into a four mile an hour wind, two meters per second. It drifts back under the parachute and lands about four hundred feet out, one hundred and twenty meters. Same flight in a twenty mile an hour wind, nine meters per second, the most the safety code allows: nearly half a mile, eight hundred meters. Tilt the rail fifteen degrees into that wind: fourteen hundred feet, four hundred and thirty meters. Thirty degrees, the legal limit, and it comes down almost on the pad. Homework step twenty-eight: change the wind and the angle until you know how far you might have to walk.

**10** `voice/10.wav`

One last thing. Before your BR-1 flies, load the G, balance it across a finger, and compare with the sim's CG. Live motor, twenty-six inches, sixty-six centimeters. A spent casing, about the motor's burnout weight, balances a little forward, at twenty-five and a quarter. If they agree within an inch, the model is honest. If not, fix the masses, run it again, then go fly.

**11** `voice/11.wav`

The one thing to remember. The pad, with the motor loaded, is the least stable a rocket will ever be. Run the sim, read four numbers, then go fly.
