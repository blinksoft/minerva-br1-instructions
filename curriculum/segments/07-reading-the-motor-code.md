# S7 — Reading the motor code

## Slot
Before lunch, while the installed motor mount sets (after step 19), if the class is ahead. Otherwise
the step 20 aft-joint wait right after lunch. Fallback: 2:00 walkthrough block.

## Objective
The cadet can decode a motor designation like G74W-6 or H128W-14 and say why average thrust, not just the
letter, decides whether a motor is safe for a given rocket.

## Live on the day
Play the video. When the pause card comes up, pause it, take two or three answers from the room, then
press play. The video gives the answer. That is the whole job; no props, no setup.

## Script outline
1. **Four parts.** Write G74W-6 on the board. The letter is the total impulse class from S6. The number is
   the average thrust in Newtons. The letter after it is the propellant type, a manufacturer's code. The
   number after the dash is the delay in seconds between burnout and the ejection charge.
2. **Read a few.** A8-3: A class, 8 Newtons average, 3 second delay, the Alpha III's motor. G74W-6: the
   BR-1's everyday motor. H128W-14: an H, 128 Newtons average, 14 second delay. H115DM-14: same idea,
   different propellant. Have cadets read the last two aloud.
3. **Why average thrust matters.** A rocket has to leave the rail fast enough for the fins to work. Rule
   of thumb: average thrust at least five times the rocket's weight, and the rocket moving about 15 meters
   per second, roughly 50 feet per second, when it clears the rail. A G40 has the same letter as a G74 but
   pushes half as hard. On a heavy rocket it may crawl off the rail and go sideways.
   - **Pause card.** On screen: "Two parts. What does H115-14 mean? And this rocket weighs about 1.5 kilograms loaded, so about 15 Newtons of weight: is a G40 enough? Is a G74?" Hold five seconds with a countdown, then a chime. On resume: H class, 115 Newtons average, 14 second delay. Five to one wants 75 Newtons: the G40 fails, the G74 just makes it. Use the real BR-1 mass when filming.
4. **Delay: the second half of the flight.** After burnout the rocket coasts up. The delay grain burns
   through and fires the ejection charge, which should happen right at the top. The right delay depends on
   the rocket and the motor together, and OpenRocket tells you (S8, S12). Some motors have a fixed delay,
   some come with an adjustable delay you drill down, and plugged motors, marked P, have no ejection at all
   because the flyer uses electronics.
5. **Every motor is certified.** The code on the label was measured on a test stand by NAR or Tripoli and
   published. That is why the RSO can trust the label and why you fly only certified motors.
