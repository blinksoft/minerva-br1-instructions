# S4 — Center of pressure

## Slot
Step 10 motor mount cure, first half. Fallback: install cure.

## Objective
The cadet can explain what the center of pressure is, why fins at the back move it aft, and how to
estimate it with a cardboard cutout.

## Live on the day
Play the video. When the pause card comes up, pause it, take two or three answers from the room, then
press play. The video gives the answer. That is the whole job; no props, no setup.

## Script outline
1. **The air pushes too.** When a rocket flies at a slight angle, air pushes sideways on everything: nose,
   tube, fins. All those pushes add up to one push at one point. That point is the center of pressure, CP.
2. **Where it is depends on shape, not weight.** A big fin catches a lot of air, so the CP sits near the
   fins. A rocket with tiny fins has its CP further forward. Nothing about the CP changes when you add a
   motor or nose weight; those move the CG, not the CP.
3. **The cardboard cutout.** Trace the rocket's side view onto cardboard, cut it out, and balance the
   cutout on a pencil. The balance point of the silhouette is a rough CP. It is where the "area" balances,
   and area is what the air pushes on. It is a conservative estimate, which is fine for us.
   - **Pause card.** On screen: "If I cut the fins off this cutout, which way does its balance point move?" Hold five seconds with a countdown, then a chime. On resume: Forward. Fins are area at the back; take them away and the area balances further up the tube. Shown on camera right after the card.
4. **What the software does.** OpenRocket does not cut cardboard. It uses the Barrowman equations, a set
   of formulas from 1966 that compute how much sideways force each part produces and where. Same idea,
   sharper answer. You will see the CP as a red dot on the rocket in S10.
5. **The trick.** Fins are at the back for one reason: to drag the CP aft. Next segment: why aft is where
   we want it.
